# THE FLIES ALWAYS FOUND US FIRST
### The Battle of Cannae — A First Person Narration
*Production Script — 60–70 minutes*

---

## PART ONE: BEFORE THE SCREAMING

---

The flies always found us before dawn.

Long before Hannibal.

Long before the screaming.

Long before Rome lost more sons than anyone believed possible in a single afternoon on a flat and unremarkable plain in the south of Italy.

The flies always found us first.

They came in from the river in the dark hours, before the watch fires had burned low, before the first pale color had appeared above the eastern hills. You would be asleep — or something close to asleep, the half-conscious drift that passes for sleep when you are lying on hard ground in a leather tent with seven other men — and you would feel it. The first one on your lip. The second on your eyelid. And then the rest of them, because wherever one fly finds something worth finding the rest will follow, and a sleeping soldier in an August camp in Apulia is, apparently, worth finding.

My name is Marcus Servilius.

Nobody remembers it now.

I was twenty-three years old, a farmer's son from outside Capua, the third of four children, a man who had done nothing remarkable in his life and who had no expectation of doing anything remarkable. I had been in the army for fourteen months. I slept in a tent with seven men whose smells I knew better than my own. I carried a scutum, two pila, and a gladius I had sharpened so many times the blade was beginning to narrow at the base.

I was nobody.

Of the eighty thousand Romans who marched toward Hannibal on the second day of August in the year we called 538 from the founding of the city — almost none of us believed we could lose.

I want you to remember that.

Almost none of us believed we could lose.

---

The tent I shared had seven other men in it.

There was Gaius — big Gaius, as we called him, not to distinguish him from another Gaius because there was no other Gaius in our eight, but because *big* was simply the first and most accurate word for him. He had arms like a butcher and a face like a man who has spent a great deal of time outdoors considering practical problems. He ate more than any man I have ever known. Not greedily — he was not greedy about anything — but with the steady mechanical thoroughness of a mill grinding, until the task was done.

There was Titus. He was forty-one and should not still have been soldiering, except that his farm had been taken by debt a decade ago and soldiering was what he knew. He had been at the Trebia. He had been at Trasimene. He adjusted his chin strap every morning before the day's first duty — tightening, releasing, tightening, releasing, three times, always three — and his chin strap was always perfectly adjusted but he adjusted it anyway. If you asked him about Trasimene he would say: wet. That was all he would say.

There was young Publius from Arretium, who was seventeen, who had lied about his age to the levy officer, who still sometimes cried very quietly in the dark when he thought no one heard.

We all heard.

We all said nothing.

And there were four others — Quintus, another man whose name I cannot now fully recall, a Samnite from the allied contingent named Vibius who spoke Latin badly and gambled extremely well, and Decimus.

Decimus was from Beneventum. He had a gap between his two front teeth that made him whistle slightly when he said his s's, which he found embarrassing and which the rest of us found quietly endearing. He was twenty-six. He had a sister he talked about sometimes — not often, just occasionally, the way you mention something that matters without wanting to seem like it matters too much. Her name was Horatia. He was teaching himself to read so he could write to her without asking a clerk.

---

The centurion of our century was a man named Gnaeus Flavius.

He had a scar from his left ear to his chin from a skirmish in Illyricum that he never discussed, and which gave his face, when he turned it a certain way in certain light, the look of a man assembled from two slightly different faces. He had been in the army since I was eight years old. He did not shout — not as a general practice. He had the particular authority of men who have learned that volume is for men who are not being listened to.

He inspected equipment without comment. He corrected with his hands more than his voice — he would simply take the thing being done wrong and do it right, silently, and hand it back. The first time he did this to me, taking my wax block and reworking the leather facing of my scutum with three strokes that achieved what my ten had not, I felt both embarrassed and oddly grateful.

He was, in the vocabulary I have assembled over years of thinking about the men who shaped me, a man you wanted to be worthy of.

This is rarer than it sounds.

---

The camp on the Aufidus River.

It is the fifth day of August by the old count, two days before the battle, and the camp is everything a Roman camp is — enormous, orderly, terrible-smelling, full of the particular noise of eighty thousand men being military at each other.

The Aufidus runs brown and low to the south. The summer has been brutal and dry and the river shows it — lower than it should be, the banks cracked at the edges, the water slow. Flies breed in slow water. There are consequently a great many flies.

The heat by mid-morning is a physical weight. It settles on your shoulders the way a pack does — not dramatically, not all at once, but cumulatively, each hour adding something, until by midday you are aware of it in every movement.

The dust is everywhere. It comes from ten thousand feet and ten thousand hooves and the mule teams and the wagon wheels and the simple fact of this many bodies occupying this much dry ground in an Apulian August. It is in your eyes. It is in the folds of your subarmalis where it combines with sweat into a paste that chafes the shoulders and neck.

It gets in your teeth.

This sounds small. It is not small. Every time you bite down, all day, every day — a faint grit between your molars. The taste of the place. The taste of the army. After a while you stop trying to spit it out, because there is always more, and you simply accept that this is what your mouth is now. Dust. That is what an army in Apulia tastes like.

The body adjusts to everything, eventually. This is the body's great virtue and its great danger.

---

The morning of the third day in camp I am assigned to a grain detail.

There is no drama in this. A tesserarius appears in the tent row before full light and names three contubernium from our century and tells us to follow him to the supply wagons. We do. We are still working our sandal straps as we walk, the hobnails of the caligae ringing on the packed earth of the via principalis.

The grain smells of dust and old wood. We fill sacks from the wagon beds — four men to a cart, the work going quickly because Gaius can hoist a full sack onto his shoulder with a single clean movement that the rest of us require two attempts and significant effort to replicate.

"You do that on purpose," I say to him, watching.

"Do what."

"Make it look easy."

He considers this. "It is easy," he says.

"For you."

He shrugs. The sack doesn't move.

The supply clerk at the wagon head is someone I recognize, but something about him is different this morning. He is moving faster than usual. Not rushing. Just not pausing. He does not look up when we approach. He marks the wax tablet and waves us through and marks the next one before we have even turned away.

I notice this.

I do not know why I notice this.

On the way back, Quintus begins complaining that his sack has been packed unevenly and the weight is wrong and someone did it deliberately. Nobody did it deliberately. It was packed in a rush in the dark before dawn. Quintus is the kind of man who interprets all inconveniences as intentional. Decimus, walking behind him, says: "Quintus. Be quiet."

Quintus is quiet for about fifty paces.

Then: "It's the distribution. The grain shifts."

"You're always just saying. Carry the sack."

---

There is a mule.

I should tell you about the mule because this is the kind of thing that happens in camps and that the histories never record and that is somehow more vivid to me now than many things that are.

On the fourth day, in the late afternoon, a mule belonging to one of the supply teams breaks free of its tether. Mules break their tethers regularly — it is one of their defining qualities — but this mule has gotten into the officers' section of the camp and has found a tent it has decided to investigate by pushing its entire head through the opening and beginning to eat something inside.

The tent belongs to a legatus I have never seen up close.

The legatus emerges.

The mule does not.

What follows is approximately twenty minutes of an increasingly frustrated senior officer attempting to remove a mule from the situation the mule has decided it prefers, while a growing audience of soldiers who have absolutely nothing better to do watches from a respectful distance. The mule is eventually removed by a slave with a bucket of water and an understanding of mule psychology the legatus clearly lacks.

The legatus disappears into his tent.

The assembled soldiers say nothing.

Then Vibius says something in Oscan that I do not understand but Titus evidently does, because he makes a sound that is the closest I have ever seen him come to a laugh.

"What did he say?" I ask.

"Nothing suitable for repeating to a man who might one day stand before a tribunal."

I look at Vibius. Vibius looks back at me with the expression of a man who has just won a bet.

Decimus has tears running down his face from trying not to laugh. He whistles slightly through that gap in his teeth every time he exhales.

We go back to our tent.

---

The night of the fourth day, I go to fill my water skin at the Aufidus and the man ahead of me in the line is someone I recognize from the second century — a big Umbrian, his name something I cannot now recall — and he is staring at the far bank of the river without moving. His skin is already full. He is just standing there with the water running past his boots.

I fill my skin. I say nothing. He says nothing.

Back at the fire, Titus is adjusting his chin strap not three times but five, and when Gaius comes back from the water detail he sits down and looks at nothing for a moment before speaking.

"Cavalry contact east of the camp," he says. "Carthaginian horse."

"Reconnaissance," Titus says. Not a question. "They're not counting us. They're reading the ground."

"What does the ground tell them."

Titus looks at the fire. "Where the dust goes when the wind comes from the east."

Publius, who has been very quiet, says: "The wind always comes from the east here."

"Yes," says Titus. "It does."

He tightens his chin strap. Releases. Tightens. Releases. Tightens. Releases.

Quintus says we could move camp, get the wind behind us. Titus says that is not for us to decide. Quintus opens his mouth. Gaius says his name. Just his name. Quintus closes his mouth.

---

The day before — and I know it is the day before, though no officer announces it, because the sacrifice fires go up along every century's line at dusk and the smell of them reaches you before the sight — there is a gambling dispute.

Vibius wins again. He always wins. Decimus loses three days' ration to him and wants it back. The argument escalates to the point where Flavius, passing through the tent row on unrelated business, stops and reads the situation.

He takes three sesterces from his own belt pouch and gives one to Decimus, one to Vibius, and keeps one.

"What's the third one for?" Decimus asks.

"For having to stop walking," Flavius says, and moves on.

Vibius laughs, a short sharp sound, and Decimus laughs too, that whistling exhale through the gap in his teeth, and it is over.

I think about this later. About how the right person in the right moment can dissolve a thing that was becoming serious. And I think about how we will not have Flavius much longer, though I do not know this yet.

---

The sacrifice.

Flavius leads us to a small fire behind the camp's east rampart as the sun goes down. A chicken. He kills it cleanly and reads the liver and says what he sees is favorable.

I watch the men around the fire as he says this.

Titus is looking at his own hands. Gaius is looking at the fire. Publius is looking at Flavius's face. Quintus is nodding too enthusiastically. Vibius has his eyes closed. Decimus is at the edge of the firelight with his wax tablet on his knee, forming letters slowly with his stylus. His lips are moving, as though he is saying each word aloud before he writes it, making sure it is right.

I watch him without meaning to.

I look at the sky above the smoke — the thick summer stars of the south, so many that the individual lights blur into general brightness at the edges of sight.

I say the prayer.

Whatever it is worth. Whoever is listening.

---

Back in the tent.

Nobody speaks for a while. This is not unusual. But tonight the quiet has weight. Titus sits with his gladius across his knees, not sharpening it, just sitting with it. Gaius is cleaning his hands with the methodical attention he gives to things that don't require attention. Vibius is turning a coin over his knuckles, end over end, the same movement repeated.

Young Publius asks if anyone has a tablet.

Nobody does.

"I wanted to write something," Publius says. "To my mother."

"Tell me what you want to say," I say. "Say it out loud."

He looks at me. "You'll remember it?"

"I have a good memory."

He thinks for a while, in the way young people think when they are trying to find the right words for a feeling they have never named before. Then he says, very quietly: *Tell her I'm all right. Tell her I've learned things I didn't know I needed to learn. Tell her the food is bad but I'm eating. Tell her I remember the way the road looks coming up the hill toward home in the evening and that I've thought about it a lot.*

He stops.

"That's enough," I say. "I'll remember it."

He lies down.

I lie down and stare at the tent ceiling in the dark and hold his words in my head and think about my own mother, and her hand on my face in the dark, and the thing I never said.

Some things don't need saying.

I fall asleep still thinking it.

---

## PART TWO: THE MORNING OF THE WORLD ENDING

---

The bucina sounds before light.

Not the assembly call. Just the watch change. But it is enough. The tent wakes the way it always does — in pieces, man by man, the accumulated routine of months.

I have not slept. It doesn't matter.

We dress in order. Titus first — always first. The chainmail hauberk, the straps at the shoulder, greaves, caligae, helmet. Chin strap: adjusted, released, adjusted, released, adjusted, released. Done.

Then Gaius. Then me. Then the others.

Publius dresses slowly. He is not procrastinating — there is no procrastination in a twelve-foot tent when seven other men are already moving. He is simply being careful. As though he wants to do it right, this once.

I watch him settle his helmet onto his head.

I don't say anything.

Outside, the sky is dark blue. Not black. Not dawn. The space between, which has no name.

---

We eat.

I cannot taste it. My mouth is doing the right things — chewing, swallowing — but the information is not reaching whatever part of me processes taste. There is dust between my teeth even now, even before the day has fully begun.

Gaius eats completely and methodically. Titus does not eat at all, which is wrong, and I push my remaining biscuit toward him and he eats it without a word, which tells me something.

The morning is very quiet — not silent, there is always noise in a camp this size — but behind all that noise there is something else.

Attention.

The whole camp paying attention.

---

The assembly takes an hour.

The sun clears the eastern hills as the last formations settle, and the light comes flat and direct and golden across the plain, and the first thing it catches is the standards.

The eagles.

Silver and gold against the dark hills, lit from the east, throwing light back into the eyes of eighty thousand men who are looking at them. I am standing in the fifth rank of the third maniple from the right. Titus is behind me. Gaius is to my left. Publius is in the rear ranks — too new for the front line. Decimus is two files to my right, his pilum held vertical, his posture good.

I look for him specifically because he is the one I can see at that angle.

He catches me looking and raises his chin once.

I raise mine.

The east wind begins.

---

A breath from the east. Light at first — the kind of breeze you welcome in August heat. Then more dust, fine grit drifting across the plain from the direction of the Carthaginian lines.

Invisible still — everything east of us is dust and hills and distance.

But somewhere in that distance is Hannibal.

And the wind is blowing from him to us.

I blink against the grit. All around me, thousands of men blinking. The dust settles between my teeth the way it always does — familiar now, the taste of this army, this summer, this plain. I bite down and feel it.

Nobody says anything.

---

We wait.

This is the part the histories pass over in a sentence or two because nothing is happening, and historians write about things that happen. But the waiting is not nothing. The waiting is the entire emotional content of the day compressed into a space too small to hold it — the dryness in the mouth, the pressure in the chest that is not quite pain, the very particular awareness of your own heartbeat.

Titus, somewhere behind me, just at the edge of hearing.

One. Two. Three.

---

A trumpet.

Not from our side.

Distant, very clean, cutting through the dust and heat like something cold. And then underneath it, something lower — drums. Not Roman drums. Deeper, less precise, with a resonance that comes through the ground as much as the air.

Beside me, Gaius shifts his weight. Just slightly.

From far to our right, our trumpets answer. The long moan of the bucina rolling across the plain.

Flavius shouts the order.

We advance.

---

I want to stay in this last second before I take you into the battle. Because these seconds are rare, and we do not know them for what they are until they are gone.

The plain is ahead of me. Dry and flat and hazed with dust. The standards moving forward. The sound of ten thousand men beginning to march in formation is not a roar — it is something more fundamental. A vibration. The ground itself communicating the fact of us.

To my left, Gaius.

Behind me, Titus.

Somewhere in the rear, Publius.

Two files to my right, Decimus.

The sun is full and white and brutal. The east wind is constant. The dust is in my eyes, my teeth, my chest.

I grip the scutum.

I grip the pila.

I walk forward.

---

## PART THREE: THE INSIDE OF IT

---

The initial contact is not what you expect.

You expect a clash — something sharp, singular, a clear boundary between before and after. What actually happens is more like a tide meeting a shore. The front line of the hastati engages the Carthaginian center and the sound and pressure of it travels back through the formation in a wave, and by the time it reaches the fifth rank I am already moving forward because the formation is moving and in a formation this dense there is no individual motion. There is only the motion of the whole.

I cannot see the enemy.

Too much dust. Too many bodies ahead of me. I can see the back of the man in front, the shoulders of the men flanking me, the occasional standard appearing through the haze and then disappearing again.

The formation pushes. I push. This is what Titus told me. Shield up, shoulder behind it, push forward, let the formation do what it was trained to do.

There is a gap here in what I remember.

Not long. A minute, maybe less. I know I threw the pila because afterward my hands were empty and the gladius was out. I know I was already in contact because my shield arm was burning in a way that takes time to arrive. But I cannot find the moment of the throwing or the moment of the drawing. They are simply gone, and in their place is the sustained pressure of the shield impact, already ongoing, as though it had always been happening.

---

The shield impact is not a single blow. It is a sustained pressure — the weight of the man ahead of me transmitted through his shield into mine, through mine into my shoulder and chest and legs, and behind that weight his whole formation leaning forward, and behind me my whole formation leaning forward. It behaves less like a fight and more like two currents meeting in a river.

Push and resist. Resist and push.

The sound is wrong. Not the sound of swords — there is that, buried under the larger sound — the sound of thousands of shields in contact, thousands of feet finding purchase on hard ground, human bodies under enormous strain. And above all of that the voices, which are not words anymore but the sounds bodies make when they are past words.

---

Time moves wrong.

In a formation this dense, in contact this sustained, time stops behaving the way it does outside. There is no sense of minutes passing. There is only the present moment — the shield and the pressure and the dust — and then suddenly it is a different moment with no transition between.

The sun has moved. I catch a glimpse of it through a gap in the dust above — much higher than it was. The pressure against my shield has been decreasing. The men ahead of me are giving ground. Slowly. But giving it.

We are winning. I am sure of this.

I push harder.

---

And then something changes.

This is the moment. This is the thing that is going to take a long time to describe because it took a long time to happen — it did not happen suddenly, it happened the way the tide comes in, which is gradually and then all at once, and by the time you are standing in water up to your chest you have long since passed the moment when you could have walked back to shore.

It is not a sound. It is not a sight. It is a feeling — the feeling of a formation, which has its own feeling when it is whole and properly ordered, beginning to change. Like a note going flat. Like a wheel beginning to wobble.

I feel it before I understand it.

---

The man to my right stumbles. Not falls — stumbles. He recovers immediately. But in recovering he steps right, and the gap that creates is filled by the press of men from behind, and suddenly the line between us is different.

I look left. Gaius is there. Gaius is always there. But the man to Gaius's left is not where he should be. And the man beyond him. I look left along the line and see — in fragments, through the dust — that the line is compressed. The gap between the maniples, which allows movement and flexibility, is gone.

Men are closer together than they should be.

And then — for a few seconds, maybe longer, maybe less — the pressure ahead of me eases. The line moves. I feel it in my boots, in the way the ground accepts my weight differently as the formation surges forward half a step, one step, two. The man ahead stumbles forward and I stumble with him and for one bright specific moment it feels again like we are winning.

Then the pressure from my left is wrong.

---

Not from ahead. From the left. A lateral pressure, new and distinct. Someone is pressing from the left. I cannot see who. I cannot turn to look.

The man behind me is breathing on the back of my neck — short, sharp, barely controlled. Through my armor, through the press of bodies, I feel something that might be his heartbeat.

The line to my left is wrong. The pressure from the left is building. I cannot hear the signals from the right flank anymore.

This last fact arrives slowly, because it requires me to notice an absence — the trumpets from the right, which have been sounding irregular calls all morning, present as a constant background. They have stopped. I do not know when they stopped.

Something is wrong.

I do not know what.

---

The man to Gaius's left goes down.

For one half-second there is a gap — a brief clear window through the dust — and I see what is on the other side.

Cavalry.

Not ours.

Moving fast. Not east-west, not toward us and away from us, but along the line of us, behind us, sweeping along the rear of where our formation should be.

The formation closes around the gap. The press of bodies tightens.

The word *cavalry* sits in my chest like a stone.

---

Then I hear it.

Somewhere to my right, two or three files over, through the noise that has ceased to be noise and become simply the atmosphere of the place — a sound that is wrong in a different way from all the other wrong sounds. A short sound. Almost nothing. The sound of a man who has been holding something together and stops holding it.

I cannot see him.

I will not see him until it is over.

But I know, in the way you know things when your body understands them before your mind does, that the sound came from where Decimus was standing.

I do not think about this.

I push.

---

The air.

The formation is so compressed — shields overlapping, shoulders touching, backs pressed against the chests of the men behind — that the normal movement of air stops. The heat each body generates accumulates. The space between the helmet's cheek guards and the face inside becomes a small hot cave of used breath. Dust in the teeth. Dust in the lungs. The taste of the whole army, of the whole terrible day, in every swallow.

The man behind me puts his hand on my shoulder.

Not pushing. Not grabbing. Just his hand there. I do not know his name. I have never known his name. To me, in that moment, in the bad air and the wrong pressure and the building sense of something deeply, fundamentally wrong — it means that there is another person there. A specific person, with a hand, behind me.

We push forward together.

---

Men fall and cannot fall.

In normal space, a man who is struck falls. The ground receives him. In this formation there is no space below. There is no space anywhere. A man two files to my right stops moving upright and begins to move differently, his head going forward, his weight shifting, and he is caught by the bodies around him. He does not fall. He travels with the formation, upright by virtue of having no room to be otherwise.

I stop watching.

I push.

---

The light changes.

This is the last coherent observation I make before the formation breaks. The light — that white August light — dims. Not because the sun has moved. Because the dust above us has thickened to the point of filtering it. The plain is darker than it was an hour ago despite the sun being higher.

We are inside the dust now.

We made the dust.

Gaius's arm comes over my left shoulder. I have told you this, and I am telling you again, because some things deserve two tellings. His arm comes over my shoulder and his hand finds the boss of my shield and he pushes — just a few inches, just enough space — and he holds it, his arm shaking with the effort of it, and I breathe.

The formation breaks.

I am on the ground.

Then I am not on the ground.

Then I am standing on the plain of Cannae in the white afternoon and the battle is over.

---

## PART FOUR: THE PLAIN AFTER

---

I stand still for a moment.

Long enough that a carrion bird lands twenty feet away and regards me with the flat professional assessment of something deciding whether I am going to be a problem. I look back at it. It decides I am not, and turns away.

This is the first thing I clearly remember seeing after the formation broke. Not the plain. Not what the plain had become. A bird. Deciding about me.

Then I start looking.

---

Gaius is six feet away, on his knees, his right hand braced on the ground, assessing his shoulder. He looks up when he hears me.

Alive.

Titus I find sitting against a stone two hundred yards north, his left arm held across his chest. When he sees me, something passes across his face — not relief, Titus's face does not easily show relief — just the brief absence of worry, which is close to the same thing.

"Marcus," he says.

I sit next to him.

We look at the plain for a while.

After a long time I say: "The cavalry."

"Both flanks," he says. "Their center pulled us in. We walked forward thinking we were winning. It bent. It didn't break. It pulled us in and the cavalry came all the way around the back." A pause. "I could see it. For a little while, from where I was."

He stops.

"Flavius?" I say.

"No." He looks at the plain. "Flavius didn't run."

Flavius didn't run. I know this because I can see his face in my mind in the moment of choosing, and that face does not have running in it.

---

We look for Decimus.

I want to tell you we found him injured. I want to tell you the sound I heard during the battle — that short, almost nothing sound — turned out to be something survivable. I want to tell you he was sitting against a wall somewhere, practicing letters, whistling through the gap in his teeth.

We walked the field for a long time.

The field in the aftermath of Cannae is not a thing I will describe in full. I will only say this: it is very large, and very still, and there is a quality to the light over it that I have not seen anywhere else before or since. A white, flat, merciless light that shows you everything and softens nothing.

We found him in the late afternoon.

He was on his back, his face turned to the side, and his expression was the same expression men have when they are deeply asleep — not peaceful exactly, but absent, turned inward, somewhere else. His left hand was open, palm up. His right was closed around the shaft of a pilum that was not his — someone else's, fallen near him, and he had grabbed it in the way the hands grab things without the mind's instruction.

We stood there for a while.

Gaius said nothing. Titus said nothing. I said nothing.

There was nothing to say about a man being gone that the fact of his absence did not already say better.

I stood there and I thought about the gap in his teeth.

That was all. That is what I thought about. Not Horatia. Not the letters. Just the gap in his teeth, and the whistling sound it made, and the afternoon with the mule when he had tears running down his face from trying not to laugh.

The mind goes where it can.

We marked where he was.

We walked north.

---

We find Publius at Canusium on the second day.

He is against a wall in the shadow with his knees drawn up. He has a cut on his arm that needs binding. He is looking at nothing.

When he sees us — all three of us, Titus and Gaius and me — he does not say anything. He looks at each of us in turn, and his face does something I do not have a word for in Latin or any other language I know. Then he looks at the ground.

"I ran," he says.

Nobody speaks.

"The left flank went and I ran. I didn't—" He stops. "I just ran."

"Publius," says Gaius, and sits down next to him.

"I ran."

"Listen." Gaius says it the way he says everything — simply, without decoration. "You're here. That's what matters. You're here and you're alive and tomorrow the sun will come up and you'll still be here. That's what right looks like from the inside of a bad day."

Publius looks at him.

Something in his face changes. Not much. But something.

---

Titus speaks to me alone that evening.

We are sitting on the wall that faces south, looking toward nothing — toward the direction of the plain we cannot see from here. The evening is cooling, the first stars appearing, the air carrying something other than heat for the first time in weeks.

He says: "You know what I keep thinking about."

"Tell me."

"The mule."

I look at him.

"The way it just stood there with its head in the tent," he says. "Like it had found exactly where it wanted to be." He almost smiles. He doesn't quite. "I keep thinking about that."

I understand this completely.

You hold onto the small things when the large things are too large to hold. The mind does this on its own, without being asked — it goes to the mule, to the figs, to the gap in a man's teeth, because these things are the right size for a human mind to carry. The other things are not. Not yet.

"Vibius paid Quintus in figs," I say.

"And Quintus complained for two days."

"Two days."

A pause.

"Flavius took a sesterce for having to stop walking," Titus says.

"That's the right amount to charge."

"It is."

We sit. Below us the city is feeding the survivors. Fires along the streets, the smell of cooked grain, people moving in and out of the firelight.

"Rome is not finished," Titus says.

He says it the way he says *the road is three miles long*. Just the fact of it. A thing measured carefully and found to be true.

"No," I say.

"We'll go back."

"Yes."

"Different next time." He looks at the stars. "Someone will figure it out. That's what Rome does. It breaks and then it figures it out." He adjusts his chin strap. One. Two. Three. "Before Trebia, before Trasimene, when I was young — there was a battle in Spain that nobody remembers now. We broke then too." He releases the strap. "Rome figured it out."

I think about this for a long time after he stops talking.

Figured it out. As though the answer is out there, findable, waiting to be found by the right person at the right moment. As though what happened on that plain is a problem with a solution rather than a wound that will scar and harden and become a kind of permanent knowledge.

Maybe those are the same thing.

I don't know. I am twenty-three years old and a farmer's son from outside Capua and I do not know very much about how empires learn their lessons.

But Titus is forty-one and has fought in three of these. And he is still here. And he says Rome figures it out.

I decide to believe him.

## PART FIVE: THE ROAD HOME

---

We march north in late August.

The formation is ragged — nothing like the column that marched south in the summer, nothing like the precision of it, the sound of it, the feeling of being part of something enormous and ordered. This is five thousand survivors organized into whatever structure the tribunes can impose on what remains, and the structure is imperfect and the march is slow.

It doesn't matter.

Moving is not retreating. There is a difference that matters enormously and that I did not understand until I felt it from the inside. Retreating is moving away from something that has defeated you, and defeat is final, and final things are ended. Moving is going somewhere. Rome is north. We are going north.

We move north.

---

Titus marches without waste.

After all those years and all those roads, his body has learned to conserve in motion what it will need later. His stride is exactly the length it should be. His posture is exactly the angle it should be. He does not look at the road. He has looked at too many roads.

Gaius walks with his right arm slightly in toward his body, protecting the shoulder without appearing to protect it. You would not notice unless you knew him. I know him.

Publius walks steadily.

This is the change I notice in him and do not comment on — he walks steadily now. He has a steadiness in his step that was not there before. Not put there by what happened on the plain. Put there by surviving what happened on the plain and still walking, and discovering that the walking continues.

On the second day of the march, Quintus falls into step beside me and says something about the march ration being short again. I tell him the march ration is what it is. He says someone is taking from it deliberately. I say nobody is taking from it deliberately, there are simply five thousand men and a finite amount of grain and sometimes the numbers don't come out evenly.

He is quiet for a while. Long enough that I think he is finished, that this will settle back into the column with all the other complaints.

Then he says: "Decimus owed me two figs."

I look at him.

His face is doing something complicated and not quite finished.

"He still owed me two figs," he says.

I don't say anything.

Quintus drops back into the column.

---

On the third day Publius asks me about the letter.

He asks if I still remember what he said.

"Tell her I'm all right," I say. "Tell her I've learned things I didn't know I needed to learn. Tell her the food is bad but I'm eating. Tell her I remember the way the road looks coming up the hill toward home in the evening and that I've thought about it a lot."

He walks for a moment.

"That's it," he says. "That's exactly it."

"When we get to Rome we'll find a clerk and send it."

A pause.

"Is it true?" I say. "That you're all right."

He thinks about this seriously, the way he does things now — not quickly, with care.

"I think I might be," he says.

---

Rome from the road.

When we came within sight of the city — the hills, the smoke of the fires, the temples catching the last light of the afternoon — Titus stopped walking.

He just stopped. In the middle of the road. Five thousand men parting around him like a river around a stone. He stood there and looked at it, and nobody said anything to him, and we stopped with him and looked at what he was looking at.

After a while he said, quietly, to no one in particular: "Thirty years I've been coming back to this."

He started walking again.

We walked with him.

The city was still there.

## EPILOGUE

---

It is two years now since Cannae.

I am still in the army. Titus is still in the army. Gaius is still in the army, his shoulder reminding him every cold morning of a day in August that neither of us needs reminding of. Publius re-enlisted after his first term ended, which surprised me and did not.

The war continues. Hannibal is still in Italy. There are things I have seen since the plain that I will not tell you about tonight.

Tonight I want to tell you a small thing.

There is a recruit in our century. Very young. The look of men who do not yet know what they are going to be asked to carry. On his third day in camp he sat down at the evening fire and said, without any approach: *What was it like? Cannae.*

I looked at him for a long time.

I told him.

It took longer than I expected. Not because there was so much to say, but because certain things required more care than others. The east wind was easy. The mule was easy. Gaius's arm over my shoulder in the compression was easy to say, though not easy to have been inside.

When I got to Decimus I stopped for a moment and did not speak, and the recruit waited without asking why, and that was good of him, the waiting.

I told him about the east wind and the dust between your teeth and the grain detail and the mule in the legatus's tent. I told him about Titus adjusting his chin strap three times, always three. I told him about Gaius's arm over my shoulder and those few inches of space and what it felt like to breathe inside them. I told him about Publius dictating a letter he was afraid he might not be able to send himself. I told him about Decimus, who was teaching himself to read so he could write to his sister without asking a clerk, and whose hand was closed around someone else's pilum when we found him, and whose name is not recorded anywhere.

The recruit listened.

When I finished he said: *Why are you still fighting?*

The honest answer is complicated and would take longer than a fire lasts. The true answer — the one underneath — is simple.

Because they are still here. Gaius and Titus and Publius. And I am still here. And while I am here there is something being preserved that I cannot name but that is worth preserving.

I don't have a better answer than that.

"Because Rome is not finished," I said.

He nodded.

He went to his tent.

---

I sat at the fire for a while longer.

I thought about my mother.

She had a way of touching my face when she thought I was asleep. She would come in and sit on the edge of my bed in the dark and set her left hand — always the left, the right never fully closed — against my cheek and hold it there. And I would lie very still and breathe as though sleeping.

I never told her that I knew.

I should have.

But some things don't need saying. Some things are said by the hand in the dark, by the silence held between two people who both understand something neither of them has found the words for yet.

There is no dust here, at this fire, in this camp two years north of Cannae. I notice its absence the way you notice a sound that has finally stopped — a relief you did not know you were waiting for. No dust between my teeth. Just the cold night air and the smell of the fire and the sound of the camp settling into sleep around me.

She is still there, on the farm outside Capua.

I am still here.

Rome is still there.

*Silence.*

*Rome did not surrender.*

*They were there anyway.*
